From a one-line idea to event-based sound matching

Why Karplus-Strong?

The Karplus-Strong algorithm turns a tiny burst of noise into a convincing plucked string. A delay line stores one period of the waveform; a two-point moving average removes a little high-frequency energy on every trip around the loop. The result is both an instrument and a compact physical explanation: pitch comes from the round-trip delay, while the gradual loss of energy creates the decay.

Original KSA. At note-on, the excitation is exactly one delay-line period long: $N = f_s/f_0$ samples. Note duration is deliberately ignored; after the burst, the loop simply rings down.

01

The original algorithm

Draw notes of any length and press play. Every onset injects a fresh $f_s/f_0$-sample noise burst into the delay loop. Because the 1983 algorithm has no note-off mechanism, changing a block's length does not change its sound.

One loop, more expressive controls

From a pluck to a playable string

Later extensions separated the physical causes that the moving average entangles. Jaffe and Smith added pluck-position and dynamics filters; Välimäki and colleagues introduced independent controls for overall decay and frequency-dependent damping. We use these ideas with a fractional delay, so $f_0$ need not land on an integer number of samples.

Extended KSA. A fractional round-trip delay controls pitch; damping, decay, pluck position, and dynamics separate the string's timbral properties; explicit note-on excitation and note-off damping turn a resonator into a playable instrument.

02

Our extended algorithm

Now note length matters. At the exact note onset the delay changes immediately to the new $f_0$ - no glide. At note-off, damping increases by 10% and decay decreases by 10%, creating a simple palm-mute gesture.

Why predict events?

A guitar note is an event, not a frame

In real guitar recordings, a string is excited and later stopped by the player. The example below is plucked and muted at approximately 3 seconds. This discrete structure motivates a model that predicts a set of events - each with its own onset, $f_0$, duration, and timbral properties - rather than unnecessarily predicting the same parameters frame by frame.

Making the loop differentiable

Two routes through the same recursive system

DDSP learns synthesis controls by backpropagating an audio reconstruction loss through the synthesiser. Prior work used a frequency-domain formulation with multi-scale spectral loss; our earlier Differentiable Karplus-Strong work used a fully time-domain implementation. Their relative behaviour had not been compared.

Frequency sampling

Fast, smooth fractional delays

The delay becomes a continuous phase shift. But the required Hermitian spectrum produces a finite, circular impulse response: if a resonant decay exceeds the FFT frame, its tail wraps around in time.

Time-domain interpolation

Native decay, no wrap-around

Fifth-order Lagrange interpolation makes delay length differentiable sample by sample. It avoids frame-based time aliasing and gives gradient accuracy comparable to frequency sampling.

The learning problem

Spectral similarity does not tell an onset which way to move

Our gradient analysis reveals the central obstacle. Multi-scale spectral loss is useful for timbre, but a shifted event often receives no meaningful directional onset gradient: it cannot reliably say “earlier” or “later.” Joint audio-and-parameter training therefore collapses unless onset and $f_0$ gradients are detached. Parameter supervision works well on synthetic events but does not yet cross the sim-to-real gap; on real audio, classical external onset and pitch detectors combined with audio losses remain the strongest KSA strategy. The tables and listening examples below let you inspect that trade-off.
